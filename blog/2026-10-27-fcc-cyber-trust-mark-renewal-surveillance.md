On 27 October 2026, the FCC's Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau
asked for comment on how the **U.S. Cyber Trust Mark** label should look,
how often certifications are renewed, and how certified products are checked
once they are on sale
([Public Notice DA 26-1162](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-1162A1.pdf)).
It is the clearest sign yet of what keeping the mark will involve.

<!-- truncate -->

The notice consults on label, renewal and surveillance recommendations
developed under the programme's first lead administrator, UL Solutions, which
later withdrew. In April 2026 the FCC selected the **ioXt Alliance** as the
new lead administrator. ioXt's technical recommendations will go out for
comment separately. Comments are due 30 days after
the notice is published in the Federal Register, with replies 15 days after
that.

## What the FCC is proposing

**Renewal every three years.** The stakeholder committee recommended five.
The FCC says five years does not keep pace with IoT threats and proposes
three. A renewal would need a new CyberLAB test report covering any new or
revised programme requirements and 70% of the remaining requirements.

**Post-market surveillance.** Certified products would be subject to ongoing
surveillance, including ad hoc surveillance, with the lead administrator
filing annual, ad hoc and post-audit surveillance reports with the FCC. If a
certification lapses, the product's cyber nutrition label would be replaced
by a notice that it "does not have a valid U.S. Cyber Trust Mark
certification", with the reason, such as failure to renew or withdrawal.

**Label design.** The FCC proposes basing the label on CTA-2120, with the
Cyber Trust Mark and a QR code on the physical label. It asks whether the
minimum font size should be 4 point or 5 point.

**Sensor disclosures.** The consumer-facing layer of the cyber nutrition label
would show whether the product collects data from camera or video, audio, or
location sensors.

## Why this matters for manufacturers

The mark is voluntary, but the proposals turn it into an ongoing commitment
rather than a one-off test:

- A three-year renewal cycle means re-testing at least once within a typical
  product's market life.
- Post-market surveillance means the shipping firmware, not only the sample
  you submitted, needs to keep meeting the criteria.
- Sensor disclosures need to match what the product really does, across all
  variants and firmware versions.

The FCC has said technical requirements and testing procedures will follow in
later proceedings. Commissioner Olivia Trusty, who now leads the rollout, has
said she hopes labelled products reach shelves within about a year
([Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2026/10/fcc-commissioner-hopes-cyber-trust-mark-products-reach-shoppers-within-year/416878/)).

## What to do next

1. If you plan to use the mark, decide whether a three-year renewal works for
   your product lines, and comment if it doesn't.
2. Map your products' camera, microphone and location features now, so the
   disclosures are accurate across SKUs.
3. Tie your support-period and update commitments to the label data, so they
   stay consistent with your EU CRA and UK PSTI statements.
4. Keep test evidence in a form you can reuse at renewal.

## The practical takeaway

The Cyber Trust Mark is moving from concept to operating rules. If it applies
to your products, plan for continuing compliance, with renewals, surveillance
and accurate disclosures, rather than a single certification.
